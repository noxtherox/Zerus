# Rooftop gardens and urban heat — research notes

These are my working notes on the Northvale University field study of rooftop gardens (Okonkwo, Arantes et al., working paper "Cooling from Above", 2025) plus some background reading. I am presenting this paper to the Heat & Health reading group on **4 December 2026**, so the notes are organised around the questions I expect people to ask. Hannah Brook, who runs the group, asked for no more than 10 slides and a clear "so what" at the end.

## 1. Why this matters

Cities are warmer than the countryside around them, especially at night and especially on calm, clear summer days. The usual explanation is a mix of dark surfaces that absorb sunlight, a lack of vegetation and open water, waste heat from traffic and air conditioning, and street canyons that trap long-wave radiation. Roofs are a large share of the exposed surface in dense neighbourhoods — the paper quotes a figure of roughly a fifth to a quarter of the horizontal area in the central districts it studied — and most of them are dark membranes or tiles that get extremely hot in direct sun.

The health side is what our reading group cares about. Top-floor flats in older buildings are often the hottest homes in a heatwave, and the people living in them are not always able to move or to pay for cooling. If greening a roof can take a few degrees off the ceiling of the flat below, that is a cheap intervention compared with retrofitting insulation or installing heat pumps. The open question, and the reason this paper is interesting, is how large the effect really is outside of controlled test plots and how well it holds up when the gardens are maintained by ordinary residents instead of researchers.

## 2. Study design

The study was led by Dr. Amara Okonkwo at the Department of Environmental Science at Northvale University, with Felipe Arantes handling the hydrology and irrigation side. They worked in the coastal city of Harlow Bay, which has a temperate maritime climate with occasional hot spells.

- **38 rooftop gardens** were compared with 38 matched conventional roofs. Matching used building age, height, roof orientation and roof material before greening.
- Measurements covered three summers: **June to August of 2023, 2024 and 2025**.
- Instruments: 152 surface temperature sensors (two per roof), 40 indoor air loggers in top-floor flats (20 under gardens, 20 under control roofs), and a small weather station on each of four representative roofs.
- Gardens fell into two types: *extensive* (shallow substrate, mostly sedum and grasses, 8–12 cm deep) and *intensive* (deeper soil of 15 cm or more, with vegetables, shrubs and small trees in planters).
- Residents recorded watering and maintenance in a shared logbook, and a short survey was run at the end of each summer.

What I like about the design is that the gardens were real community projects rather than test plots. What I like less is that buildings were not randomly assigned: residents applied for a small grant to build the garden, so the "treated" buildings had more motivated residents to begin with. The authors acknowledge this in the limitations section and try to adjust for it, but it cannot be fully removed.

## 3. Main findings

The headline number is that rooftop gardens reduced the **average peak afternoon roof surface temperature by 4.2 °C** compared with the matched conventional roofs, averaged over all three summers. That is the number everyone will remember, so it is worth unpacking.

- The effect depended strongly on depth. Roofs with soil of 15 cm or more averaged a reduction of about 5.3 °C, while shallow sedum roofs averaged about 2.1 °C — roughly half the overall figure.
- Indoor air temperature in top-floor flats was **1.6 °C lower** in the afternoon (between 14:00 and 18:00) under gardens than under control roofs.
- At night the difference was small, about 0.4 °C, and not statistically significant in 2023.
- During the hottest week of the study, a heatwave in the second half of July 2024, the surface difference reached **6.8 °C** on the best-performing intensive gardens.
- Well-watered gardens performed much better than dry ones. In weeks when residents skipped irrigation, the cooling effect on intensive roofs dropped by around a third.

The indoor result is the one that matters for health, and it is more modest than the surface number. A difference of 1.6 °C is still meaningful — it can be the difference between a bedroom that is uncomfortable and one that is dangerous for an elderly person — but it is important not to oversell surface temperatures as if they were room temperatures.

## 4. Costs, water and maintenance

The paper gives fairly detailed cost figures, which I want to put on a single slide.

| Item | Extensive roof | Intensive roof |
|---|---|---|
| Installation cost | about €85 per m² | about €140 per m² |
| Typical garden size | 60 m² | 45 m² |
| Irrigation in July | 6 litres per m² per week | 12 litres per m² per week |
| Volunteer hours per month | 3 | 10 |

The water figure is a real issue. Harlow Bay had a hosepipe ban for two weeks in August 2025, and several intensive gardens lost plants during that period. Felipe Arantes's section argues that rainwater storage on the roof (small tanks of 1,000 to 2,000 litres) could cover most of the summer demand for extensive roofs but not for intensive ones.

Maintenance turned out to be the weak point. By the third summer, **9 of the 38 gardens** showed a clear decline in plant cover, usually because the most active volunteer had moved away or lost interest. The authors suggest that city grants should include a small paid maintenance budget for at least five years instead of only funding installation.

## 5. The social side

The end-of-summer survey had a response rate of 64%. Residents in buildings with gardens were more likely to say they knew their neighbours by name and more likely to report sleeping better during hot weeks. I am cautious about these results: the sample is self-selected, the questions were not validated instruments, and people who built a garden together are obviously going to feel more positive about the building. Still, several residents wrote that the garden gave them a cool place to sit in the evening, which is a benefit that sensors do not measure.

A smaller point that came up in the comments: two buildings reported problems with insurance and with the landlord's approval, which delayed construction by almost a year. That seems like a practical barrier worth mentioning to the group.

## 6. Limitations

- **One climate zone.** Harlow Bay is temperate and maritime. In a hot, dry city the water demand would be much higher and the trade-offs could be very different.
- **Self-selected buildings.** As noted above, residents applied to take part.
- **No energy data.** The study did not look at electricity or gas bills, so we cannot say whether flats under gardens used less cooling.
- **Short time span.** Three summers is not long enough to see the full life cycle of a roof membrane or how gardens age.
- **Working paper.** It has not been peer reviewed yet, and some of the statistical details are only in the supplementary material.

## 7. How it fits with other reading

The figures are broadly in line with what I have seen elsewhere. A review by Ibarra and Chen (2022) that I read for last month's session reported surface reductions between 2 °C and 5 °C for most green roofs in temperate cities, with deeper substrates at the top end of the range. The Northvale study is on the high side for intensive roofs and in the middle for extensive ones. The indoor reduction of 1.6 °C is slightly higher than most earlier work, which may be because the study focused on older buildings with poor roof insulation, where the roof has a bigger influence on the flat below.

Another comparison worth making is with "cool roofs" — simply painting roofs white or using reflective membranes. Those are much cheaper (often under €20 per m²) and need no water, but they do nothing for biodiversity or for residents' wellbeing, and they can increase glare for taller neighbouring buildings. I think the honest conclusion is that cool roofs are the default and gardens are worth it where residents want them and there is a plan for water and maintenance.

## 8. Questions for the reading group

1. Is a 1.6 °C indoor reduction enough to justify public funding, compared with other heat interventions?
2. Should grants require rainwater storage for intensive gardens?
3. How should the city fund maintenance beyond the first few years?
4. What would a version of this study look like in a hot, dry city?

## 9. My takeaways

- The surface number (4.2 °C) is real but easy to misuse; lead with the indoor number when talking about health.
- Depth and water matter more than anything else.
- Maintenance funding is the most practical policy lesson.
